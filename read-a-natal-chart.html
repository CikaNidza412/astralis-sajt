How to read a natal chart from the wheel

Follow a fictional natal chart from its zodiac degrees to whole-sign houses and a worked example of a planetary aspect.

Start with three positions

Imagine a chart with the Sun at 15° Taurus, the Moon at 17° Virgo, and the Ascendant at 10° Cancer. These placements are invented for practice, so you can follow the example without sharing a birth date. Find the Sun and Moon on the wheel, then find the Ascendant, often labeled ASC. In a typical chart drawing the Ascendant sits on the left side, where the chart marks the eastern horizon.

Copy the three placements from the chart’s written position table if it has one. The degrees next to each sign are the positions within that sign. Each sign spans 30° in the tropical zodiac used for this example. Signs, houses, and aspect lines can share the same wheel, which makes the picture busy. The written positions give you a reliable place to start before tracing the shapes.

Follow the house numbers

This example uses whole-sign houses. Cancer contains the Ascendant, so all of Cancer is the first house. The Ascendant itself stays at 10° Cancer. Count forward through the signs, with Leo as house two and Virgo as house three. Continue the same count through Pisces as house nine, Aries as house ten, and Taurus as house eleven. The Moon in Virgo therefore falls in the third house and the Sun in Taurus falls in the eleventh.

The chart’s house setting matters when you compare those results with a different wheel. In the equal-house system that starts at the Ascendant, the first house would begin at 10° Cancer and the second at 10° Leo. A point at 6° Leo would fall in house one with that setting and house two with whole-sign houses. The point would remain at 6° Leo in both charts. Read the house label before interpreting an apparent discrepancy.

Calculate the angle yourself

To compare the Sun and Moon, convert both to degrees measured from 0° Aries. Taurus starts at 30°, so 15° Taurus is 45°. Virgo starts at 150°, so 17° Virgo is 167°. Subtract 45° from 167° and you get 122°. In astrology a trine is centered at 120°, so this example sits 2° from exact. A chart using a 3° allowance would draw the trine. A chart using a 1° allowance would leave it out. The allowance is called an orb.

Two signs of the same element can look like an obvious match on the wheel. Degrees give a more specific answer when the method calls for a measured aspect. For example, 2° Taurus and 28° Virgo are 146° apart. A narrow orb around 120° would exclude that pair even though their signs are the same as in the first example. This is a good reason to check the degree numbers before relying on a colored line or a sign label.

Write one interpretation you can examine

Within modern Western astrology, the Sun is often read as a symbol of direction, the Moon as a symbol of habitual responses, the third house as a setting for everyday learning and exchanges, and the eleventh as a setting for groups. One possible reading of the example connects practical routines with participation in a group. A concrete reflection question would be whether a regular learning habit helps you contribute to a project with other people. You might answer with an example from a class, a club, or a work team.

Keep the chart observations and the reflection question in separate lines. The numerical result, 122°, follows from the example degrees. The meaning assigned to it comes from an astrological convention and personal reflection. To practice on another wheel, identify the zodiac and house settings, record two positions, measure their separation, and write down one question that can be answered from experience.
