Synastry in practice with two fictional charts

Work through three synastry aspects, see how an orb changes the result, and turn the interpretation into a conversation question.

Put each placement with the correct person

Synastry is an astrological practice that compares placements from two natal charts. A bi-wheel puts one chart on the inside and the other around it. In this exercise, Mira has Mercury at 12° Gemini, the Moon at 29° Pisces, and Mars at 15° Taurus. Luka has Mercury at 13° Aquarius and Venus at 2° Aries. These are fictional placements for checking the arithmetic by hand, so birth details are unnecessary.

Before tracing lines, mark each person’s placements on paper and check the legend of any wheel you use. Each comparison needs one position from Mira and one from Luka. An aspect between Mira’s Mercury and her own Moon belongs to her natal chart. A chart that uses separate colors for each person can be easier to scan, but keep the written names beside the positions when you record your results.

Measure the shorter arc

Express the positions as longitudes around one circle, beginning at 0° Aries. Mira’s Moon at 29° Pisces is 359°. Luka’s Venus at 2° Aries is 2°. Their numerical difference is 357°, so the shorter distance is 360° minus 357°, which equals 3°. An astrologer using a 3° orb or wider would include a Moon–Venus conjunction in this example. Two points can sit in neighboring signs and still be close together because the signs meet at the same boundary.

Now compare their Mercury placements. Mira’s 12° Gemini is 72° and Luka’s 13° Aquarius is 313°. Their difference is 241°, so the shorter distance is 119°. The exact trine angle is 120°, giving an orb of 1°. To try a square, compare Mira’s Mars at 45° with Luka’s Mercury at 313°. The shorter distance is 92°, or 2° from the 90° square. With a maximum orb of 2° inclusive, this example includes both Mercury contacts and leaves out the Moon–Venus conjunction. At 3° inclusive it includes all three.

Turn the interpretation into an open question

In modern Western astrology, writers often connect Mercury with conversation and thinking, and Mars with initiative or directness. A symbolic reading of the Mercury trine might prompt Mira and Luka to ask which discussions feel easy to them. The Mars–Mercury square might prompt them to ask how they each handle a quick decision. Their answers could easily coexist. Two people may enjoy talking through ideas while disagreeing on how quickly to act on them.

Imagine that they are deciding how to spend a free Saturday. One person may want to reserve a train seat before prices change. The other may want to confirm whether friends can join first. A useful question is when each person needs a decision and what information is missing. This question invites both people to describe the situation. It works without assigning a fixed personality trait to either person.

Know what a compatibility number leaves out

An astrology calculator can turn chosen contacts into a single display score. The result depends on how the calculator defines each contact, which orb it allows, and how much weight it gives each contact. In the example above the score could change simply because the orb went from 2° to 3°. A reading’s percentage cannot tell you the chance that a relationship will succeed. Relationship outcomes involve what the people do and what they experience together.

For a practice reading, use fictional charts or ask before entering another person’s birth details. If a birth time is missing, leave out precise angle comparisons and check how far the Moon may have moved before interpreting a close contact. The exercise succeeds when you can show how an angle was derived, explain the convention behind the reading, and ask a question the people involved can answer for themselves.
